Viva protocol 3 MINUTES = 4–6 QUESTIONS

01

One captain routes

Harshit takes every question first and hands off by name: "That's Sankalp, our index statistician." Never two people answering at once — it reads as a team that doesn't know who owns what.

02

25 seconds, then stop

Number first, then the reason, then silence. If they want more they'll ask. Talking past the answer is how you hand them the next objection.

03

Never invent a number

If you don't have it: "We haven't measured that yet — the design is X, and we'd validate against Y." A judge forgives a gap. Nobody forgives a fabricated statistic.

04

Reframe, don't defend

Agree with the true part, then redirect. "You're right that dynamic pricing is brutal — that's exactly why a single monthly check is meaningless, and why we use multilateral methods."

05

Say the institution's name

ONS, MoSPI, DGCA, the CPI Manual, Rule 135(2). Every named source converts an opinion into a citation. Government panels score citations.

06

Never say "we didn't have time"

Say what exists and what's designed: "Lanes A and B are running; Lane C is built and rate-budgeted." Scope honestly, frame it as roadmap, not shortfall.

Question bank 44 QUESTIONS · OWNER TAG = WHO ANSWERS IN THE ROOM

Tier 1 — Basics

ANYONE ON THE PANEL CAN ASK THESE
1
What is VIMAAN, in one sentence?
A real-time airfare price index for India. We collect fares automatically from airline and travel portals across 500+ routes three times a day, and reduce them to one audit-grade index number MoSPI can feed into the CPI 2024 airfare component.
Harshit
2
What problem are you actually solving?
MoSPI still collects the CPI airfare by hand from online platforms, and DGCA's Tariff Monitoring Unit checks only 78 routes, once a month. That's one price from a surface with thousands of cells — thin, lagged about 60 days, and biased.
Soumya
3
How is this different from MakeMyTrip or Skyscanner?
They sell tickets; we produce an official statistic. Their output goes into a shopping cart, ours goes into the CPI. Concretely: a methodology endpoint, an SDMX-JSON feed and a reproducibility hash — none of which a fare-search product has any reason to build.
Harshit
4
What is a price index, in one line?
A number measuring how a fixed basket's price has changed against a base period set to 100. So 118.7 means fares are 18.7% above the 2024 base — not that a ticket costs ₹118.70.
Sankalp
5
Why airfares specifically? Isn't it a small item?
Because it's the worst-measured item in the basket. Air transport sits inside Transport, weighted 9.43% in CPI 2024, and it's where the sampling method is furthest from market reality — one monthly manual check against fares that move 15–20 times a day.
Shruti
6
Who actually uses this?
Four users. MoSPI takes the index into CPI. RBI gets a cleaner inflation input. DGCA gets 500+ routes monitored with surge alerts instead of 78 by hand. Citizens get a public lead-time index showing the real advance-purchase penalty.
Shruti
7
What does "augmenting the CPI" mean?
We don't replace the CPI. We replace one input to it — the manually collected airfare price — with a daily, quality-adjusted, weighted index, delivered in SDMX-JSON, the format MoSPI's systems already exchange statistics in.
Daksh
8
So your deliverable is a dashboard?
The dashboard is the shop window. The deliverable is the API: /v1/index/national, /v1/sdmx/CPI_AIRFARE, /v1/methodology/{id}. Every response embeds coverage, imputation share, revision flag and reproducibility hash. Nothing is published without provenance.
Daksh
9
How often does it update?
Collection three times daily — 02:00, 11:00, 19:00 IST — to catch intra-day revenue-management moves. Daily route price is the median of intra-day observations. Index publishes daily; the official bulletin is monthly.
Bhumika
10
Where does the number 78 come from?
DGCA's Tariff Monitoring Unit — it monitors airfares on 78 routes, monthly, by staff opening airline websites. PIB / MoCA, PRID 1810467. That's our published baseline to beat.
Bhumika

Tier 2 — Data, legality & engineering

THE OBVIOUS FOLLOW-UPS
11
Isn't web scraping illegal?
Two of our three lanes don't scrape at all. Rule 135(2) of the Aircraft Rules, 1937 requires airlines to publish their established tariff — Lane A reads mandated public disclosure. Lane B uses licensed distribution APIs under contract. Lane C reads publicly displayed prices, honours robots.txt, rate-budgets, zero personal data. We explicitly refuse CAPTCHA-solving and fingerprint spoofing.
Bhumika
12
What if a portal blocks your collector?
The index survives on any one lane. Lanes A and B are statutory and contractual — they can't be blocked. We hold a rolling buffer, so even total collection failure for days doesn't move the monthly number; it moves the coverage percentage, which we publish.
Bhumika
13
Why not just ask the airlines for their data?
Rule 135 already makes them publish it. Voluntary sharing would be aggregated, delayed and self-reported — three things a statistician can't audit. We capture what the consumer actually faces, at the moment they face it.
Bhumika
14
Do you store personal data? What about DPDP?
Zero personal data. We collect fares, not passengers — no names, no PNRs, no user-linked searches, no real-session cookies. There is no personal data in the pipeline, so DPDP obligations around personal data don't attach. Documented in COMPLIANCE.md.
Bhumika
15
Where do your index weights come from?
DGCA Monthly Domestic Traffic Statistics — route-wise passengers in the base period. A route's weight is its share of base-period passengers, so Delhi–Mumbai counts far more than a thin regional route. That sits inside the CPI 2024 Transport structure.
Sankalp
16
How much data per day? Can you handle it?
500 routes × 60 dates × 8 buckets is over-sampled, so we run a PPS rotating panel driven by DGCA traffic — roughly 150,000 observations a day. Single machine on TimescaleDB. Storage is the cheap part; the index maths is the hard part.
Soumya
17
Why PostgreSQL + TimescaleDB rather than plain SQL or Mongo?
Everything we query is a time series over a route × date × lead-time cube. Timescale hypertables give continuous aggregates and compression, so a 24-month index query runs in milliseconds. We also keep Parquet + DuckDB for analytical replay — that's what makes reproducibility cheap.
Soumya
18
What is your medallion architecture actually storing?
Bronze — immutable raw snapshot with SHA-256, collector version, timestamp. Silver — normalised fare observations, IATA-coded, tax-inclusive, currency-parsed. Gold — the price-surface cube the index engine reads. Bronze is what makes any number re-derivable years later.
Soumya
19
What happens when a website changes its layout?
Parsers are config-driven, not hardcoded — selectors live in config. A schema-drift detector compares field presence and value distributions against the last known-good run and alarms per lane on an SLO. Meanwhile the other two lanes keep the index alive.
Bhumika
20
Does this cover international routes?
Domestic first — that's where the CPI weight and DGCA's mandate sit. Architecture is identical for international; the changes are currency handling, lead-time buckets (ONS uses 6/3/1 month long-haul) and the weight source. Configuration change, not a redesign.
Soumya

Tier 3 — Statistics & method

THE QUESTIONS THAT DECIDE THE SCORE
21
Why a geometric mean (Jevons) instead of a simple average?
Arithmetic means are upward-biased when prices are volatile, and airfares are the most volatile thing in the basket. The international CPI Manual prescribes Jevons for elementary aggregates precisely for this. An arithmetic mean would import that bias straight into headline CPI.
Sankalp
22
What is chain drift, and why does it matter here?
Chain a price index period-by-period over prices that bounce between buckets and the index fails to return to its start when prices do — it ratchets upward. On dynamic airline pricing that manufactures several percent of inflation a year that never happened. On flat simulated fares, a naive daily-chained index drifts up +12 index points.
Sankalp
23
Explain GEKS-Jevons.
Instead of chaining 0→1→2→…→t, GEKS computes every possible bilateral comparison within a window and takes the geometric mean of the paths from 0 to t through each intermediate period. Because it's symmetric and uses all pairs it is transitive — drift cancels by construction. Jevons is the bilateral formula underneath.
Sankalp
24
Why a 25-month window, and what is splicing?
25 months = two full seasonal cycles plus one, so every month is compared against its own season. An index must never revise published history, so when the window rolls we mean-splice: take the new window's movement and link it onto the existing series. Published NSO practice — ONS and Statistics Netherlands both do this.
Sankalp
25
What is hedonic quality adjustment — concretely?
We regress log fare on stops, log duration, departure slot, refundability, baggage and carrier, giving the price of each attribute. Example: IndiGo replaces a nonstop with a one-stop — average fare falls, but that's a worse product, not deflation. The model re-prices everything to a fixed reference spec, so the drop reads as a quality change.
Sankalp
26
A ticket bought in August for a December flight — which month's inflation?
International practice (ONS FOI-2023-1164) attributes the fare to the month of departure, not purchase, because the index measures the price of consuming the flight. We attribute on that use basis and publish an acquisition-basis series in parallel so MoSPI can choose. Almost nobody handles this, and it materially changes the answer.
Sankalp
27
How do you seasonally adjust when Diwali moves every year?
X-13ARIMA-SEATS with moving-holiday regressors for Diwali, Holi, Eid, Christmas and school-vacation windows. A plain seasonal filter assumes the spike falls in the same calendar month every year — for Indian festivals it doesn't, so it mis-attributes the spike and leaves a phantom seasonal in the wrong month.
Sankalp
28
CPI base is 2024 — but you have no 2024 fares. What's your denominator?
Honest answer: we backcast it from DGCA published route yields, archived fare data and web-archive snapshots, and publish the base as provisional with a confidence band that narrows as live data accumulates. We flag it rather than hide it — a published uncertainty is defensible, a silent assumption isn't.
Sankalp
29
Listed fare versus what people actually pay — isn't your index biased?
Yes, and we correct rather than pretend. Portals show the cheapest bucket; realised yield differs. We estimate k = realised yield ÷ listed median from DGCA route revenue and passenger data, apply it as a slow-moving level correction, and publish uncertainty bands. For an index, a stable level bias largely cancels — what matters is the movement.
Sankalp
30
Laspeyres, Lowe or Young — which and why?
Upper level uses a Lowe/Young index with DGCA base-period passenger weights, which is what CPIs actually run in practice. We also publish the classic Laspeyres form alongside for direct comparability with MoSPI's existing method — and explain the gap rather than quietly presenting one number.
Sankalp
31
What if a route has no fares on a given day — missing cells?
A two-way fixed-effects imputation — route FE + date FE + lead-bucket effect — so a missing cell fills from the route's own level and the day's market-wide movement. We publish the imputation share with every number, and anything below a coverage threshold is marked provisional.
Sankalp
32
What is Time-Product-Dummy, and why run both it and GEKS?
TPD is a regression — ln p_jt = μ_t + λ_j + ε — where the time dummies μ_t are the index, with product fixed effects absorbing quality. It's a second, independent multilateral estimator. We run both because agreement between two different estimators is evidence; divergence is a flag that something in the data is wrong.
Sankalp
33
How would you prove your index is actually right?
Four tests. One: benchmark against MoSPI's own manual airfare series over overlapping months. Two: cross-lane divergence tests — statutory vs API vs portal — with a flag threshold, so we never silently average disagreeing sources. Three: leave-one-route-out sensitivity on the DGCA weights. Four: a published revision policy.
Daksh
34
Why three collections a day rather than one, or twenty?
Revenue-management systems reprice through the day, so one snapshot samples an arbitrary point on that path. Three — morning, midday, evening — captures the shape at acceptable request cost, and we take the median, robust to a single anomalous scrape. Twenty adds load without materially reducing variance.
Sankalp
